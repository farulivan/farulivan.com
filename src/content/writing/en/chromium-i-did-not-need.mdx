The biggest performance win available to you is often not in your code. It is a
dependency you stopped noticing.

## What the graph looked like

A small service of mine sends reminders over WhatsApp. Its entire job is to
read short text messages and occasionally write one. Steady state, it held
between 0.75 and 0.85 GB of memory, all day, whether anyone had sent it
anything or not.

I had looked at that number many times without seeing it. It was the number the
service used. It had always been the number the service used.

What made it visible was writing the sentence down: _a program that reads text
messages is holding most of a gigabyte._ Said out loud, that is obviously
absurd. Read off a dashboard, it is just a line.

## Where it was going

The bot talked to WhatsApp by driving a headless Chromium. A real browser,
logged in, kept alive indefinitely. That is a legitimate way to automate
something that has no other interface, and for a long time it was the only way
this one had.

It was also the cause of every outage the project had ever had. Not most of
them. Every one. A browser is a large, stateful thing to keep running for
months at a stretch, and when it failed it did not always fail loudly. It would
sit there, process alive, doing nothing, until somebody noticed the reminders
had stopped.

So the browser was costing me eight hundred megabytes and all of my downtime,
and delivering something I could get another way.

## The fix was a deletion

WhatsApp's protocol is reachable over a WebSocket. Speaking it directly meant
no browser, no page, no rendering engine. A socket, a handful of message types,
and the same functionality.

Memory went under 100 MB. That is more than an 85% cut, and it arrived in a
change whose diff was mostly red. The single largest improvement I have made to
that codebase was removing something from it.

## What I take from it

I do not think the lesson is "never automate through a browser". Sometimes
there is no other door.

The lesson is about where I look first. My instinct on a memory problem is to
go inward: profile the code I wrote, find the retained object, tune the cache.
That instinct assumes the cost is mine. Often the largest single cost in a
service is one dependency doing a job far bigger than the one you need from it,
and no amount of tuning your own code touches it.

Before optimising a component, it is worth asking whether the component should
be there. That question has a much better payoff than the one I usually ask,
and it is the cheaper one to answer.
