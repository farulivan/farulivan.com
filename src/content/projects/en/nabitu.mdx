## The problem

Nabitu is a shariah investment platform: small businesses raise money, and
people who do not want to earn interest invest it. By the time I arrived it
existed as an MVP — enough to prove the idea, not enough to run a financial
product for years.

I was the only frontend engineer, and stayed the only one for three years.
That single fact shaped everything else. There was nobody to hand a hard
problem to, and every convention on the front end was one I set and then had to
live with.

## Rewriting seventy pages

The rewrite took the better part of two years and moved the whole product onto
Next.js. Seventy pages, in three parts that behave nothing like each other:

- **The landing site**, 12 pages, public and indexed, where the only thing that
  matters is that it loads fast and says the right thing.
- **The member dashboard**, 37 pages, where people look at money they have
  actually committed. Slow is bad; wrong is unacceptable.
- **The admin panel**, 21 pages, used by a handful of internal staff who need
  density over polish and are unforgiving about a workflow that takes one click
  more than it should.

Treating those as one application would have been the easy mistake. They share
a design system, an API layer and a type contract, and almost nothing else.

## Two brands out of one codebase

Nabitu is the Indonesian brand. Zeed Sharia is the same platform for an
international audience — a different name, a different visual identity, a
different language, and money in a different currency.

Both ship from one codebase. What varies is more than a theme: copy,
formatting, currency handling and the parts of the flow that only apply in one
market. Getting that seam in the right place was the most interesting
engineering problem in the project. Put it too shallow and you are maintaining
two products; too deep and every component grows a brand conditional and nobody
can read it any more.

## Faster, by looking at the data first

The rewrite roughly doubled page performance. Very little of that came from
micro-optimising components. Most of it came from reading how the API actually
behaved — what it returned, when, and how often the interface asked for things
it already had — and then changing the fetching patterns to match. The
component work followed the data, not the other way round.

## Working across the stack

The backends were Go and Laravel, owned by other people. My part was defining
the API contracts with them, and — when something broke across the boundary —
reading enough of their code to say precisely where it broke rather than filing
a ticket that said the page was wrong.

That is cross-layer fluency, not backend ownership. I consumed those services;
I did not build them.

## Outcome

The platform serves more than 3,000 investors across both brands. The support
team's feedback after launch was that the experience and usability had improved
markedly — which, from the people who hear every complaint first, is the
review I trust most.
