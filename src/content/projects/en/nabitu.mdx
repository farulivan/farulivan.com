## Where it started

Nabitu and Zeed Sharia are shariah investment platforms: places to invest
without earning interest. When I joined, the designs were done in Figma and the
backend work was halfway along. The frontend was mine to build from scratch,
first in plain React.

I was the only frontend engineer, and stayed the only one for three years.
That shaped everything else. There was nobody to hand a hard problem to, and
every convention on the frontend was one I set and then had to live with.

## Rebuilding seventy pages

With the React frontend live, I spent the better part of two years rebuilding
it on Next.js. Seventy pages, in three parts that behave nothing like each
other:

- **The landing site**, 12 pages, public and indexed, where the only thing that
  matters is that it loads fast and says the right thing.
- **The member dashboard**, 37 pages, where people look at money they've
  committed. Slow is bad; wrong is unacceptable.
- **The admin panel**, 21 pages, used by a handful of internal staff who need
  density over polish and are unforgiving about a workflow that takes one click
  more than it should.

Treating those as one application would have been the easy mistake. They share
a design system, an API layer and a type contract, and almost nothing else.

## Faster, by looking at the data first

The rebuild roughly doubled page performance. Very little of that came from
micro-optimising components. Most of it came from reading how the API behaved:
what it returned, when, and how often the interface asked for things it already
had. The fetching patterns changed to match, and the component work followed
the data, not the other way round.

## Working across the stack

The backends were Go and Laravel, owned by other engineers. My part was
defining the API contracts with them. We were a small team, and I was curious,
so I learned the Laravel side well enough to read it, follow its logs and see
how the pieces connected. When something broke across the boundary, I could say
precisely where, instead of filing a ticket that said the page was wrong.

Then I took it to the engineer who owned it, and we worked it out together. I
changed backend code myself only when something was urgent, with that engineer
approving the change and monitoring it. That's cross-layer fluency, not backend
ownership.

## Outcome

The React frontend I built from scratch serves more than 3,000 investors. Once
it replaced the earlier version, the support team's feedback was that the
experience and usability had improved markedly. From the people who hear every
complaint first, that's the review I trust most.
